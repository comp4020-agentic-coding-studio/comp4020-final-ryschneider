# Your app

Margins is a place for a small club to read a text together, and it treats the text as the thing that matters. Everything else in the app is subordinate to the page.

For Margins, "good" means the page stays readable and the group's attention becomes visible without anyone having to read a thread. Most social software measures itself by how much gets said; Margins measures itself by a narrower test: after an evening of five people reading the same chapter, can you open the page and see where the group lingered? Annotations are made in the margin, live, as people read, and they're short. As they age they collapse into attention marks, a quiet heat along the text showing which passages drew people in, while the notes themselves fade back unless someone returns to them.

That is the concrete difference from a discussion board or a comment thread. A thread is ordered by time and grows without limit, and its reward is more replies. Margins is ordered by position in the text, and its state decays. A note's warmth spreads to the surrounding passage and fades if nobody touches it, so what you see at any moment is where the group's attention currently is, not an archive of everything ever typed. Two people reading the same paragraph see each other's marks appear live in their own colour, which is the part a comment section can't give you: the sense of reading in the same room, and the sense of whose attention you're looking at.

I didn't get here by studying social products. The sources that shaped the decision were older and more physical: the marginalia in a shared library book, and the dog-eared copy a reading group passes around, where the creases show you where everyone got stuck. Margins is that copy, made live, and now made yours: an account keeps your name and your colour across every book you open, on whatever device you open it from, rather than reintroducing yourself in every room.

A stranger should be able to land on a room already in use, watch a mark appear and spread, and understand within a minute what the app is for: they see the text first, they see where other people paused, and they feel pulled to leave a note of their own. "A chat app with extra steps" is what Margins would be if the notes stayed in a stream, if the text shrank into a sidebar, or if nothing ever faded.

What's changed since the test above was first written is who gets to start a room. A club used to be whatever I'd seeded by hand from the library; now any reader with an account can pick a text, give the room a title, and set how many people it's for, and that room is as real as one I seeded myself. The choice of what to read and how big the room is now belongs to the people actually reading, not to whoever holds the deploy key.

I am still deliberately not trying to be good at several things: moderation, a considered mobile experience beyond a basic responsive layout, scaling past a single machine, or rich text. Those matter for a real product. For this project the only question is whether one shared text, read live, by people who can now be the same person across visits, produces a page that tells the truth about a group's attention. If it does, the rest is worth building. If it doesn't, none of the rest would have saved it.
